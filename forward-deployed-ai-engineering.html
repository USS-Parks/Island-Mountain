Forward Deployed

Learn the workflow first. Build the system after.

Show up, shut up, and learn the work the way they run it, not the way the SOP describes it. Then fill in the gaps in conversation.

Technology that ignores expertise gets ignored by the experts.

The argument

Tome-level knowledge barely lives in the documentation.

So how would it live in a language model? The knowledge that decides whether a deployment works has been in someone's hands for fifteen or twenty years. It isn't written down anywhere you can point a retrieval pipeline at. The only way to get it is to go sit with the person who has it.

The last twenty-five months of confident proselytizing from tech CEOs implied that expertise like this is easy to replicate. It isn't. Watching someone do a job they've done for two decades is worth more than any amount of requirements gathering, and it's the part almost nobody budgets for.

Who we sit with

The expertise is already in the building.

Every industry has them, and every job classification has its own version: the person who has run this particular work for fifteen or twenty years and carries the whole thing in their head. Some of who we've sat with, and who we expect to:

  • The senior paralegal who knows which intake questions matter and which are on the form because they've always been on the form
  • The records clerk in fiscal who can tell you which form gets filled out wrong every single time
  • The compliance officer who knows where the risk actually lives, which is rarely where the risk register says
  • The charge nurse who runs the floor at three in the morning
  • The export control officer who has read every license on the wall
  • The docketing clerk who has never once missed a deadline and can tell you why
  • The surveillance shift lead who spots the pattern well before the system flags it
  • The enrollment officer who knows the families and the history behind every file
  • The control room operator who hears the fault before the alarm board admits it
  • The BSA analyst who can tell structuring from a bad week
  • The registrar who knows which exception is legitimate and which is someone's favor
  • The claims adjuster who can separate fraud from plain bad luck on the first read
  • The lab manager who keeps chain of custody honest when nobody is checking
  • The underwriter who prices the things a model will never see
  • The dispatcher who knows which call is actually the emergency
  • The shop foreman who knows which spec gets waived and who has to sign for it

The list doesn't end, and it isn't ours to write. Yours are already on payroll, and the most essential window in a deployment opens while we're sitting next to one of them. That's the part you can't outsource, can't shortcut, and can't buy in a box.

How a deployment runs

Four steps, and the first one isn't technical

01

Shadow

We sit with the people who do the job and watch the work run. Its cadence, its battle rhythms, the workarounds nobody documented.

02

Translate

The real workflow becomes a system design. Forward deployed engineering is mostly translation and synthesis, and this is where it happens.

03

Build

Now the stack gets sized. Hardware and models chosen to fit the work, never the other way around.

04

Hand Off

Your people run it. A deployment that leaves behind a team thinking differently is the one still working a year later.

Why this works now

The hardware question got boring, and that's the good news.

A $7,000 laptop now runs open-weight models in the frontier weight class. That's the second revolution arriving on schedule, and it moves the hard part of this work off the spec sheet and onto the workflow. When the substrate stops being the constraint, the constraint becomes whether anyone bothered to learn how your organization actually operates.

So we stopped leading with the gear. The stack is swappable: a bench of DGX Sparks, RTX 5090s, RTX PRO 6000 Blackwells, on up to H100 and H200 capacity, sized to what the workflow study turned up. The models are swappable too, open-source and open-weight, changed out as the frontier moves, with no lab holding a meter. Already own hardware? We wire the serving stack, set the air-gap configuration, and train your team on gear we didn't sell, because on-premise AI failing in regulated industries helps nobody.

What you're left with

Build around the experts and the deployment defends itself.

A system shaped around how someone already works gets used. A system that ignores them gets quietly routed around until it's shelfware with a budget line. That's the whole difference, and it gets decided in week one, not at go-live.

What stays behind when we leave: an independent on-site deployment your organization owns outright, the Woven Security & Governance Fabric holding every action to an identity and a receipt, and a team that understands the thing well enough to change it.
