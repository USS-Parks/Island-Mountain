## 26: Institutions Keep Buying AI Infrastructure Twice

**Publish:** Monday, September 14
**Source idea:** 29
**Icon:** `icons/masters/linkedin-26-buying-twice-icon.png`
**Form:** F3 walkthrough
**First comment:** Run the two-purchase math on your own workload before the first invoice: https://islandmountain.io/sovereign-cost-worksheet.html?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=authority-2026&utm_content=p26

### Long-form article

Imagine an institution’s first AI purchase, a composite I’ve assembled from too many real ones.

January: a capable workstation gets ordered, because starting quickly felt decisive and a quote was easier than a specification. It arrives, it impresses, and a pilot’s born.

April: the real workload shows up uninvited. Concurrency was a guess, and the guess was low. The chosen model wants more memory than the box carries. Storage growth got ignored, security needs isolation the machine can’t offer, and the rack it was destined for turns out to lack the power anyway.

August: a second, larger procurement begins, this one resembling what Discovery would’ve specified in January. The workstation gets wheeled to a corner and rechristened a development machine, which is a dignified title for the production system it was supposed to be.

Now rewind the tape and let me change two decisions.

In the alternate January, the small purchase still happens, but it’s declared a prototype out loud, with a learning objective, a retirement path, and no production costume. Its job: prove the workflow’s useful, confirm the data exists and is governed, learn how people review output, discover what quality the task demands. Prototypes answer questions; that’s their entire economy, and a bounded one can be the smartest money in the whole program.

The alternate August then buys production against evidence instead of hope: measured input and output sizes, observed arrival patterns, models evaluated on the institution’s own task, a facility survey with numbers in it, a written expansion rule stating what changes at which threshold. And the prototype gets its second life planned before its first invoice, as a test environment, a training system, or an isolated development node.

Same two purchases, radically different meanings. In the first timeline, the institution paid production prices for an experiment then acted surprised that production still wasn’t built. In the second, it paid experiment prices for answers and production prices for a system.

Compute keeps improving while stalled projects age, so buying before the gate’s passed converts uncertainty into depreciating metal. I’d rather the metal wait for the evidence. It always waits better than the budget does.

#AIProcurement #AIInfrastructure #CapacityPlanning #OnPremAI

### LinkedIn summary post

There’s a machine in a corner of many institutions with a story to tell. Bought as production, demoted to development, replaced within a year by what the specification should’ve been.

The double purchase is a sequencing choice, not fate.

A prototype declared honestly, with a learning objective and a retirement plan, is cheap tuition: it’s proof of the workflow, the data, the review habits, the quality bar.

Production then gets bought against measurements. Observed concurrency, real document sizes, models tested on your task, a facility survey, an expansion rule in writing.

We give the small machine a planned second act too: test bench, training rig, isolated dev node.

Learning on modest hardware’s wisdom. Paying production prices for an experiment, twice, is a habit worth breaking on purpose.

## 27: What I Refuse to Build

**Publish:** Tuesday, September 15
**Source idea:** 25
**Icon:** `icons/masters/linkedin-27-refusal-lines-icon.png`
**Form:** F4 doctrine clauses
**First comment:** The doctrine these refusals come from, in full: https://islandmountain.io/the-island-mountain-doctrine.html?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=authority-2026&utm_content=p27

### Long-form article

A company’s mission shows up most clearly in the revenue it declines. Island Mountain carries five permanent refusals, and I’ll state them the way I state them to customers, plainly and in the first person.

I won’t automate a consequential human verdict. The machine may assemble evidence, retrieve policy, organize records, draft the paperwork, and point at inconsistencies all day long. Denying a claim, determining enrollment, issuing a clinical order, disciplining an employee, exercising governmental authority: those stay with the qualified person and the institution, structurally, so there’s no button to press even under deadline.

I won’t surveil the people whose knowledge an institution wants preserved. Capture built to help the next shift understand the work becomes poison the moment it doubles as hidden productivity scoring or a quiet disciplinary file. Staff know what’s collected, why, who sees it, and how to correct it, or the capture doesn’t run.

I won’t monetize customer data. Records, prompts, corrections, outputs, and every model derived from an institution’s material follow that institution’s law and agreement. Technical access is a job requirement, and it manufactures zero ownership. There’s no secondary business hiding in anyone’s archive, and there never will be.

I won’t sell a deployment that requires Island Mountain forever. Support continuing by choice is a healthy relationship; dependency by design is a leash with an invoice attached. Credentials, documentation, procedures, and working knowledge finish the engagement in the institution’s hands.

I won’t recommend equipment before Discovery. A catalog converts uncertainty into a bigger box, so the work, the data, the governance, the facility, and the acceptance tests set the specification instead, every time.

These refusals cost real money, which is precisely their value as information; a principle that only survives when it supports the sale was a price list with a values page stapled on. They also turn out to be excellent engineering, since each one removes a dangerous design path before anyone walks down it.

My suggestion for evaluating any AI supplier, us included: ask what they won’t build even with budget approved and the customer asking. Specific answers reveal doctrine. Vague ones reveal a price.

#ResponsibleAI #HumanAgency #DataSovereignty #AIEthics

### LinkedIn summary post

Five things Island Mountain won’t build, at any budget:

Verdict automation. I’ll ship software that assembles evidence; qualified people keep every consequential decision, structurally.

Expert surveillance. Knowledge capture that turns into covert scoring poisons the knowledge and the experts both.

Data monetization. Your records and the models derived from them aren’t ours to sell. Access we need for the work creates no claim we get to keep.

Permanent dependency. The engagement ends by design, with your staff running, changing, and teaching the system.

Pre-Discovery equipment picks. The workload sets the spec, never a catalog page.

Each refusal has cost us revenue, which is exactly why it’s informative.

A question worth asking every supplier: what won’t you build, even if we pay? Time the pause before the answer.

## 28: What "The Moving Ceiling" Argues

**Publish:** Wednesday, September 16
**Source idea:** 1
**Icon:** `icons/masters/linkedin-28-preparedness-agents-icon.png`
**Form:** F7 essay
**First comment:** For preparedness conversations, exercises, or the paper itself, reach us here: https://islandmountain.io/contact.html?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=authority-2026&utm_content=p28

### Long-form article

When a planning methodology can’t describe a threat, the plans built on it inherit the blindness. That inheritance is the subject of my paper, “The Moving Ceiling,” and I’d like to lay out its argument in plain terms.

Emergency management plans against a ceiling: the worst credible threat the current method can represent. CPG 201 draws that ceiling with real discipline, forcing jurisdictions to define impacts, capabilities, targets, and gaps concretely enough to support accountable planning, and I’ll grant that value fully before criticizing the structure.

The paper names three structural faults. The taxonomy sorts threats into natural, technological, and human-caused bins, while an AI-augmented attack runs through all three at once, human in origin, technical in execution, disaster-scale in cascade. The update cycle runs on years while the capability it’s tracking moves on months. And the assessment’s a snapshot, taken once, of an adversary that adapts after meeting your first defensive response.

Federal evidence points the same direction. A December 2024 GAO report on AI and critical infrastructure found that the sector risk assessments it reviewed didn’t fully estimate both likelihood and consequence, limiting agencies’ ability to choose mitigations against measured risk. That finding doesn’t prove my thesis, and I don’t claim it does. It does establish the measurement gap as more than one practitioner’s frustration.

To make the argument trainable instead of theoretical, I designed Operation Cascadian Shadow; it’s an HSEEP-style exercise. The scenario: a major seismic event used as cover while an adversary poisons digital operations and public trust with manipulated information, compromised systems, and fraudulent communications. Players discover they can’t win by out-computing the attacker. They win by making the digital attack irrelevant to physical operations: life-safety functions that run under manual control, authentication that travels out of band, verification chains made of people, and authority delegated before the incident. An analog root of trust, in other words, standing under whatever the network’s doing that day. The package is a designed test environment; I don’t claim validated outcomes until after-action records exist.

For practitioners, the actionable version fits in a paragraph. Inventory the life-safety functions that depend on networked systems. Decide now how each gets verified and operated when the information environment can’t be trusted. Assign the people, the authority, and the exercise hours.

The ceiling will keep moving, and arguing with that fact wastes time we could spend building the floor.

#EmergencyManagement #CriticalInfrastructure #AIThreats #ContinuityPlanning

## 29: What Production Teaches a Governance Layer

**Publish:** Thursday, September 17
**Source idea:** 3
**Icon:** `icons/masters/linkedin-29-production-governance-icon.png`
**Form:** F6 spec with commentary
**First comment:** The governance layer these receipts and seam tests come from: https://islandmountain.io/lamprey-woven-security-governance.html?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=authority-2026&utm_content=p29

### Long-form article

Controlled tests flatter governance layers. Identity present, policy consulted, credential issued, tool run, receipt written, everyone home by five. Production’s where the flattery stops, so I’ll publish the two artifacts we hold builds against.

First, the receipt schema. “Task completed” is a caption, and captions don’t survive investigations. A receipt that earns the name connects:

- The initiating identity and the task it claimed.
- The policy decision, by id, that permitted this specific action.
- The credential’s scope and lifetime as issued.
- The tool request and the tool’s response, separately recorded.
- Any human intervention, with who and when.
- The final disposition, plus distinct identifiers for every retry and partial attempt, so an investigator can tell the third try from the first without archaeology.

Second, the seam tests, run before any launch I’ll sign:

- Expire the credential in the gap between policy approval and execution.
- Deliver the same tool result twice and prove nothing acts twice.
- Interrupt receipt storage mid-write and check what the record claims afterward.
- Change policy while a run is in flight.
- Remove the agent’s owner and watch the orphaned authority get handled.
- Point two agents at one record and audit the collision.

The commentary these artifacts teach. Policy has to fail closed without stranding the institution: an unreachable decision service means the agent doesn’t coast on remembered permission, while a documented, narrow, reviewed emergency path keeps mission work recoverable. Budgets need more dimensions than money, because a loop that’s cheap in dollars can still flood a queue, hammer a destination, or send the same message forty times; calls, time, tokens, records, and destinations each get their own ceiling. And human operations never leave the control plane. Somebody owns policy changes, reviews unusual grants, reads receipts, rotates authority, and decides when an automated path returns to service, all without holding broad permanent access to do it.

One discipline note, since we’re in public: none of the above cites an unnamed customer’s production incident, and none of it needs to. When a deployment authorizes disclosure, the evidence can speak with names attached; until then, the standard doesn’t need borrowed war stories.

Architecture declares intentions. Receipts and seam tests audit them after the easy path’s gone.

#AgentGovernance #ProductionAI #IdentitySecurity #Auditability

### LinkedIn summary post

Plus the rule that makes failure survivable: policy fails closed, while a narrow, visible, reviewed emergency path keeps recovery possible.

Diagrams state intent. These two artifacts audit it; that’s their whole job.

## 30: Europe's Sovereignty Tiers Deserve an American Reading

**Publish:** Friday, September 18
**Source idea:** 23
**Icon:** `icons/masters/linkedin-30-eu-sovereignty-icon.png`
**Form:** F8 dissent
**First comment:** When the classification says full custody, this is what we build: https://islandmountain.io/air-gapped-ai-inference.html?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=authority-2026&utm_content=p30

### Long-form article

“Europe just banned American clouds.”

That’s the headline version making the rounds, and it’s wrong in a way that wastes a genuinely useful document, so let me argue with it properly.

What the European Commission’s 2026 Cloud and AI Development Act proposal describes is a voluntary framework with four assurance levels, climbing from baseline transparency toward stronger control over operations, software supply chain, jurisdictional exposure, and protection from third-country interference at the top. Criteria, not a blacklist. The legislative text may still change, and reducing it to a trade-war slogan misses what it offers, which is a working vocabulary for a question American institutions keep fumbling.

The question: how much custody does this workload deserve? Sovereign-cloud marketing answers with a slogan. The tier structure answers with graduation, and graduation’s the insight worth importing. Public drafting and low-impact analysis tolerate one custody model. Tribal enrollment records, privileged legal work, export-controlled technical data, health records, and critical-infrastructure operations deserve another, and pretending one platform posture fits the whole portfolio is how institutions end up sovereign in the press release only.

Here’s the American reading I recommend, no legislation required. Classify each workload before choosing its platform. Then interrogate the dimensions the EU proposal usefully separates: who holds the data, who can administer the service, which legal entities and jurisdictions are involved, whether the provider can reach plaintext, how software enters the environment, which dependencies require outside connectivity, and whether the institution keeps operating if the relationship ends. Write the accepted dependencies down instead of hiding them under the word sovereign.

Some workloads will land on transparency plus contractual controls, and that’s a fine, honest landing. Others will demand customer-held keys, dedicated operations, locally governed administrators, isolated update procedures, or full custody of hardware and software. The classification should make the call, and it shouldn’t be outvoted by fashion.

One caution keeps the tiers honest: custody still has hard edges underneath the gradations. Another company either can or can’t legally administer your system. Data either does or doesn’t leave your chosen boundary. You either can or can’t operate after the contract dies. Tiers exist to make those facts visible, and the moment they’re used to blur them instead, you’ve bought a slogan with extra steps.

#DigitalSovereignty #CloudGovernance #EUAI #DataCustody

### LinkedIn summary post

The EU’s cloud sovereignty proposal keeps getting covered as a ban on American providers. Read the actual structure instead; it’s more useful than the headline.

Four voluntary assurance levels, from baseline transparency up through operational, supply-chain, and jurisdictional protections. A vocabulary for matching custody to consequence, which is the conversation most institutions skip.

My advice to American buyers: borrow the frame today. Classify the workload, then ask who holds the data, who can administer the platform, whether plaintext’s reachable, how software enters, and what happens the day the contract ends. Record the dependencies you accept.

Low-impact drafting can share custody comfortably. Enrollment records, privileged matters, and controlled technical data probably can’t.

Underneath every tier, the binary facts don’t move. Tiers should expose them, never launder them.

#DigitalSovereignty #CloudGovernance #DataCustody
