Discovery Before Config: Why We Won't Size Your Stack Until We Sit With Your Experts

Basho Parks

I don't size a stack from a first call, a budget ceiling, or a slide that already picked the box. Config without Discovery is inventory theater: a quote wearing an engineering badge. At Island Mountain I sit with the people who run the work first, then I size. Discovery can kill the project on purpose. That's the gate, and it's the only honest order.

Inventory theater has a tell

Somebody wants a recommendation before anyone's watched a Tuesday. The ask arrives dressed as efficiency. "Just tell us what to buy so we can get it on the capital plan." I've heard the same sentence from counties, clinics, firms, and shops that already know better in every other part of their operation. They wouldn't let a vendor pick a records system from a phone call. They'll let one pick an inference stack, because the market trained them to shop hardware the way they shop software licenses.

A config that isn't downstream of the work is a catalog entry with your logo pasted on. It can look complete. Power draw, rack units, a model family, a training day on the calendar. None of that tells you whether the pain shows up often enough to justify iron, whether the data's lawful to use, or whether the person who owns the verdict will still own it after the box is humming. That's inventory. The theater is pretending it's a design.

I wrote the shorter version of this gate already: Discovery can kill the project. This post's the sequencing corollary. Kill the project before you buy the shelf, or don't call the purchase a deployment.

The chain doesn't start at Configure

The work runs in one order: Immerse, Discover, Configure, Onboard. Configure never goes first. The homepage states that sequence in four steps, and I hold the same order in the room.

Immerse is the on-site stretch with senior people, managers, and founders. Not a discovery deck. I watch the work the way they run it: cadence, battle rhythms, the workarounds that never made the SOP. If I haven't sat in the room, I don't know the job. A call can tell me whether the conversation's worth having. It can't tell me how a crowded Friday really moves.

Discover's the map. Use-cases already latent in the organization, most of them never said out loud. That's where the six clauses get applied, and where a project earns a yes, a not yet, or a no. Mapping isn't a workshop that produces a slide. It's a written account of who feels the pain, what data the work sits on, which number already counts as success, and who keeps the last word.

Configure's the stack purchase recommended for what Discovery turned up, then deployed and configured together, on-site, with room to grow. The recommendation doesn't exist until the work has been seen. There's no catalog on this site for that reason, and the FAQ says the same thing without the long argument. Anyone offering you a standing spec before they've watched the work is offering you their inventory.

Onboard's the part the industry treats like a tour at the end. It isn't. A deployment nobody can run is a very expensive shelf. Education is the deployment, and onboarding is part of the system, not a leftover hour after the crate is opened.

Skip Immerse and you configure against a story. Skip Discover and you configure against hope. Skip Onboard and you've configured a monument.

Why I refuse to size from the first conversation

Guessing up front is the most common way on-premises AI deployments fail. The box arrives, it's wrong for the work, and nobody in the room wants to be the one who says so. I've watched institutions live with that silence for years. The hardware isn't the embarrassment. The unused workflow is.

Anyone who sizes your system on the first call is sizing their inventory. I don't do that, and I don't pretend a scoping call is a substitute for sitting with your experts. A call can surface whether the pain is real. It can't tell you how many people hit the system at once, how big the documents run, which decisions stay human, or whether the closet that would hold the machine has the power and the cooling to carry it.

If you want a cost picture, read the on-premises comparison after the work is mapped, not before. If you need the data to stay in the building, air-gapped inference is a custody question, not a checkbox on a quote. Both of those pages get misused as shopping lists. They're not. They're what you consult once Discovery has named the job.

The six clauses, because a gate that can't fail is theater too

Discovery at Island Mountain runs under six clauses. I hold every engagement to them. A sales process that can't fail can't be trusted, so these exist to make failure possible. The live short form is on Discovery Can Kill the Project. Here's the same gate, opened far enough that a buyer can use it without me in the room.

One. The pain must be frequent and owned. A problem that appears twice a year, or belongs to nobody who feels it weekly, doesn't justify infrastructure. It justifies a calendar reminder. Frequency's the first honesty test. Ownership's the second. If you can't name the person who'll feel the system on a crowded day, you don't have a buyer. You've got a committee.

Two. The data must be available, governed, and lawful to use. "The data exists in theory" means a governance project is hiding inside the AI project. It goes first, or everything waits. I've stopped letting an AI engagement smuggle a records cleanup that nobody funded. If counsel, records, or the sovereign owner hasn't cleared the corpus, there's no stack to size.

Three. Success gets measured in numbers the institution already respects. Enthusiasm isn't a metric. A baseline you can't state before the build is an improvement you can't claim after it. If the institution already reports a number to a board, a commission, or a court, that's the number. I don't invent a dashboard to make the deployment look busy.

Four. The human keeps the verdict. When a business case only works by removing a consequential decision from a qualified person, the engagement stops there. That's the line I don't move for any budget. Computation can assemble context. It doesn't get the last word, and I won't sell a system that needs it to.

Five. The facility and the operating team must be able to carry the system. Power, cooling, administration, recovery. A deployment the institution can't sustain is a countdown, not a capability. I've killed more projects on a closet survey than on a model eval, and I don't apologize for it. If nobody on staff can recover the box on a Saturday, you haven't bought a capability. You've bought a pager that rings my number.

Six. Risk stays proportionate to the measured gain, with blast radius sized honestly, including what one bad output does downstream on a crowded day. A draft that a clerk can throw away is a different animal than a filing that leaves the building. I size the blast radius in public, with the people who'll live with it.

Three verdicts come out of that gate, and all three carry value. "No" protects staff from another pilot that arrives with executive attention and departs leaving cleanup. "Not yet" names the dependency worth solving on its own schedule. "Yes" produces an owned workflow, a baseline, authority boundaries, and acceptance tests written before procurement.

A sequencing corollary I'd defend anywhere: make the first deployment dull, frequent, measurable, and small in consequence. Draft the report. Assemble the record. Early credibility funds the harder second problem.

NIST's AI RMF fronts context for the same reason this gate exists. A model can't be judged apart from its task, its setting, and what its failures cost.

Right-size is a place, not a product name

When the work finally earns a yes, I still don't hand you a SKU. I talk in places. The smaller system is often the honest answer, and the public language stays that simple on purpose.

A bench's a workstation-class footprint: one person or a small team, documents that fit a desk, a room that already has the power. A closet rack's the next honest step: a shared service, a locked closet or a small closet-adjacent rack, a handful of concurrent users, recovery the existing IT staff can run. A data hall's the large answer: known concurrent demand, measured batch loads, or a regulated environment that needs redundancy from the first hour.

Those three words are the whole public menu. Not because I'm coy. Because a named product line invites shopping by the seller's appetite, and I don't sell appetite. Headroom you can't justify is a purchase cost, a power draw, a cooling burden, and an operating obligation that starts billing on day one. Growth might arrive someday. The invoice already has.

I won't publish a dollar figure to make this easier to shop. Quotes get scoped after the work has been seen, human to human. If a page on this site ever starts looking like a price list, it's drifted, and you should call me on it.

What I need from your experts

I need the person who feels the pain weekly, not only the person who approved the initiative. I need a walkthrough of a real case, including the ugly one. I need the number the institution already reports, even if it's embarrassing. I need the room that would hold the machine, with the breaker labeled. I need the name of the human who keeps the verdict after go-live.

If those people aren't available, that isn't Discovery. That's a briefing. I can do a briefing. I won't pretend it's Discovery, and I won't size a stack from one.

That's also why I don't lead with a hardware conversation on the homepage. The homepage states the sequence. This post's the argument for why that sequence isn't a sales delay. It's how you keep from buying a shelf that looks like a system.
