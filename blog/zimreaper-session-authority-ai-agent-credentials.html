ZimReaper Ran With a Human's Session. So Does Your AI Agent.

Basho Parks

ZimReaper never held a privilege of its own. TA488's payload ran as JavaScript inside the browser of a user who'd already logged into their Zimbra mailbox, delivered through CVE-2025-66376, a stored XSS that fires when the message opens. It inherited the session: CSRF token, autofilled password, 2FA scratch codes, 90 days of mail via Zimbra's own export. Every call was legitimate and correctly authenticated, so there wasn't anything for EDR to catch. It worked that way for eight months against Ukrainian government agencies, US defense contractors and nuclear installations before the joint CISA, NSA and FBI advisory landed.

Before leaving it called CreateAppSpecificPasswordRequest and minted a password named ZimbraWeb, granting IMAP, POP3 and SMTP without two-factor. Then comes Monday: patch to 10.1.13 or 10.0.18, force a password reset, re-enroll everyone in 2FA. Three correct actions, none of which touches an app-specific password; surviving a reset's the entire reason app passwords exist. The patch closes the delivery path and doesn't revoke a thing.

Zimbra shipped the fix November 6. CISA didn't add the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog until March 18, with an April 1 deadline for federal agencies. These were on-premises boxes; on-prem puts the patch in your hands, and the failure mode with it.

Strip the tradecraft off and you've got a process that ran with a human's session authority and provisioned itself a durable non-human credential before anyone noticed either thing. That's the default shape of an AI agent. One under a user's OAuth grant produces calls your logs attribute to the user; let it create service accounts, API keys or MCP credentials and it leaves artifacts that outlive the session and never reach an offboarding checklist that's written for people who quit.

The controls that catch this never lived in the model; they're deterministic and sit in the infrastructure around it: identity, egress, metering, audit, and a kill switch. An agent's credential should expire with the task it was issued for and leave a receipt: who approved it, what it could reach, when it died. A prompt is a request. A revoked credential is a fact.

Grep /opt/zimbra/log/audit.log for CreateAppSpecificPassword and revoke anything named ZimbraWeb (the patch won't do it for you), then list every non-human credential you've got and the human who approved each one; that's the first artifact I build on any deployment.
