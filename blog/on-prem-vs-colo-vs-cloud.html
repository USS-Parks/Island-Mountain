On-Premise vs. Colocation vs. Cloud AI: A Decision Framework for Regulated Industries

Basho Parks

The Decision That Shapes Every Audit After It

The worst AI infrastructure decision a regulated shop can make is picking the wrong framework for the decision itself, not the wrong vendor.

Most organizations buy this like office equipment: cheapest, fastest, least demanding of staff. That's fine for laptops. It fails once the system will process clinical trial data under 21 CFR Part 11, patient records under HIPAA, CUI under CMMC, tribal member data, or a law firm's privileged files, because there it's a compliance posture you'll defend in every audit and breach notification for the life of the system.

In 2024 and 2025, FDA frequently cited missing audit trails, corrupted electronic records, and lack of system validation, and it's reasonable to predict that once AI use is widespread, regulators will scrutinize how companies control AI data flows. (IntuitionLabs) That last part's a prediction, and I'd bet the house on it.

Start With the Data, Not the Architecture

One question comes before all the others: where does your data live, where does it travel, and who touches it while the AI's working on it?

Answer honestly and your workloads fall into two piles. The first touches identified subject data, protected health information, or anything your IRB protocol governs, where access runs through institutional approval of each user, per-dataset credentials, encryption, and strict segregation of datasets. (PubMed Central) The second's de-identified data, published literature, synthetic datasets, and administrative content with no regulatory classification. Nobody's losing sleep over that one.

Most labs run both at once, and the fatal move's one architecture across both. The architecture follows the classification, never the other way round.

On-Premises: Maximum Control, Maximum Responsibility

On-premises means your hardware lives in your facility and your data never crosses your perimeter while it's processed. The deciding factor for a Part 11 lab's the audit trail. FDA inspectors keep writing observations for unvalidated spreadsheets, uncontrolled user access in lab systems, and missing audit trails. (IntuitionLabs) On your own hardware you control the logging, access controls, validation documentation, and change management; nothing rests on somebody else's SOC 2 report or a BAA clause negotiated at eleven at night.

The trade-off's real and I won't soften it: upfront spend on hardware, power, and cooling; slow scaling, since adding GPUs means buying and installing; and you own the stack down to the drivers. (HBS) On-premises wins when the workloads carry your most regulated data at steady volume and you've got the IT competency or can build it. Put the literature review on it and that's overkill; I'd say so on the first visit.

Colocation: Physical Control Without the Whole Burden

Colocation's the middle option, and regulated shops underuse it. You own the hardware and control the data; a third-party data center supplies power, cooling, physical security, and network. Your data stays in systems you physically control, which eases residency, HIPAA and GDPR compliance, and third-party access concerns. (WhiteFiber) Your encryption keys, audit logs, and validated system documentation stay yours; the facility sells you a physical envelope and a network, and that's all.

You give up latency to internal systems and take on third-party coordination during incidents. Colocation's the right call when compliance demands physical sovereignty over the hardware and your own building can't carry the power and cooling load, which describes plenty of mid-sized labs.

Cloud AI: Legitimate for Some Workloads, Treacherous for Others

Public cloud remains unmatched for elasticity and rapid prototyping, but stable, predictable workloads often run more cost-effectively on-premises or in private clouds. (HBS) I'd rather quote that straight than pretend the cloud hasn't got a case; its case is early experimentation, episodic burst training, and demand nobody can predict.

Cloud AI passes the compliance test when three conditions hold at once: the data's de-identified or synthetic; the provider offers a properly scoped Business Associate Agreement and, where it applies, a FedRAMP-authorized environment; and your validated system documentation accounts for the inherited controls. All three; two isn't a passing grade.

That case collapses the instant identified clinical data or IRB-governed records enter the workflow, and since most labs can't guarantee they never will, you need deliberate governance rather than "we'll use the cloud for AI" or "we'll avoid it entirely."

Match the Architecture to the Workload's Risk Class

Not all data carries the same risk, so match the architecture to each workload's profile. (TechTarget) Identified subject data, validated GxP systems, and IRB-governed datasets belong on on-premises or colocation infrastructure under your direct control, period. De-identified research data, internal analytics, and dev and test systems with no production PHI can live in the cloud under the right vendor agreements. The gray zone, where classification's uncertain, demands documentation that says why you chose what you chose and what compensates for the residual risk; write it down before somebody asks.

FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on AI in drug and biologic submissions proposes a risk-based framework for model credibility: define the model's context of use, assess risk by model influence and decision consequence, then document a credibility assessment plan. (IntuitionLabs) It's the same shape as an infrastructure framework: where the context of use touches regulated decisions with patient safety or data integrity consequences, the infrastructure underneath belongs under the most direct control you can get.

Build the Map Before You Build the Stack

We don't start with a product and tell you it fits. Discovery happens on site, next to the QA auditor reading executed batch records line by line for a correction that never got single-lined and initialed; that knowledge lives in her head, not the SOP. Only after your classification, obligations, workload profile, and capacity are mapped do we recommend a stack, deploy it together on site, and stay to onboard your team. I don't know which of the three you are yet, and neither do you.

If you're weighing AI infrastructure under Part 11, HIPAA, CMMC, tribal data governance, or attorney-client privilege, classify your workloads by regulatory risk tier before talking to any vendor, us included: which datasets carry which compliance obligations, and where your facility can carry hardware. That map's what turns every vendor conversation after it from speculation into work; for help drawing it, reach us.

Summary: There's no single right AI architecture for a regulated shop; it's a workload-by-workload call matching infrastructure control to data sensitivity and compliance risk. On-premises carries your most regulated workloads, colocation gives physical sovereignty without the facility burden, and cloud fits only de-identified or synthetic work.
