HIPAA Technical Safeguards Checklist for Local AI Deployment

Basho Parks

Updated October 28, 2026: The HIPAA Security Rule update is still a proposed rule; the Unified Agenda lists July 2027 for final action; the rule in force is 45 CFR Part 164, Subpart C.

The practices OCR fines are the ones that didn't know they were supposed to try. A physician got tired of finishing notes at ten at night, billing found a tool that drafts appeals, and now there's software touching ePHI nobody's written into a risk analysis. The Security Rule doesn't care whether it's a person or an algorithm; it applies in full. (HIPAA Journal)

In 2025, 76% of OCR enforcement actions carried a penalty for a risk analysis failure, fines running $25,000 to $3 million, and OCR's confirmed its 2026 priorities won't change much. (HIPAA Journal)

The Checklist: 45 CFR §164.312

  1. Unique user identification: physician, coder and AR rep each query under their own logged ID. (AccountableHQ)
  2. Emergency access procedure: a break-glass protocol you own, naming who gets in when authentication's down. (eCFR)
  3. Automatic logoff: inactivity timers you set at application, OS and network. (AccountableHQ)
  4. Encryption: at rest and in transit, under keys your privacy officer can produce. (HHS.gov)
  5. Audit controls: a tamper-evident log of every access and export the Woven Security Fabric keeps on your hardware. (AccountableHQ)
  6. Integrity controls: hashes and a write-protected output log you verify yourself. (AccountableHQ)
  7. Person or entity authentication: MFA (proposed as mandatory) at application, network and workstation. (eCFR, Duo Security)
  8. Transmission security: TLS between workstations and the inference server, both ends inside your walls. (AccountableHQ)
  9. Business associate agreement: every vendor touching PHI signs one banning training on patient data; a model you run doesn't need one. (Texas Medical Association, Mcneeslaw)
  10. Risk analysis: rewritten to name model poisoning and prompt injection on a machine that's down the hall. (Ogletree)

HIPAA's written for an organization that controls its own systems; local inference makes every item above provable because the machine and its logs are yours to produce. Large health systems run cloud AI on purpose and keep a vendor risk team watching it; an independent practice doing the same takes on a supervision job it can't staff.

Build it where you can put a hand on it.
