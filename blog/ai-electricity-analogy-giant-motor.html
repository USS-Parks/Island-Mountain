AI Is Electricity in 1895, and Everybody's Shopping for the Biggest Motor in the County

Basho Parks

The Biggest Motor in the County

A factory in 1895 ran on one steam engine, line shafts down every bay, and leather belts dropping to each machine, the whole building drawn around the engine's reach instead of the work's flow. When central power arrived, most owners pulled the steam engine, bolted one giant electric motor in its place, and kept every shaft and belt where it stood. Cleaner, quieter, somewhat cheaper to run. Output didn't much notice.

Warren Devine's shafts-to-wires accounting puts electric motors at under five percent of American factory drive in 1899 and near eighty by the late twenties. Productivity's the curve that stings: Paul David's dynamo paper traced it flat for close to four decades, because the first wave changed the power source and nothing else. The revolution didn't come until owners put a motor on every machine, unit drive in the trade's term, and let the layout answer to the work.

The Giant-Motor Play, Rebooted

I'd put most of the AI market at the giant-motor stage. The standing play, 44 months into the chatbot era: buy seat licenses and lay them over an unchanged workflow. MIT's NANDA group measured the wreckage last summer: 95 percent of enterprise generative-AI pilots returned nothing measurable to the P&L, and the diagnosis was generic tools draped over workflows that didn't change shape. The five percent getting paid embedded the system inside one workflow and let the workflow reorganize around it.

There's a category error underneath that play: AI as a chatbot you subscribe to. That's meeting electricity and concluding the product is lamps. The chatbot's a fine lamp; I burn one all day. But a subscription seat can't hold your workflow, your data, or your audit trail.

Unit drive in 2026 is a governed model inside the claims operation, drafting the file note as the demand package lands and teeing the subrogation trigger before the adjuster's diary comes due. That work's data isn't allowed to leave the building, so the rewiring question and the sovereignty question are the same question. A motor bolts to the machine; it doesn't phone the utility for permission per revolution. The AI equivalent is open weights running on hardware you own, governed by controls that sit in your paddock rather than a vendor's.

You Don't Get Four Decades

David dates the productivity payoff to the 1920s; an owner who slept through the first twenty years still caught the wave. AI doesn't extend the courtesy. METR's task-length work has frontier agents doubling the length of work they can finish roughly every seven months, and across 2024 and 2025 the doubling ran closer to four. Months, compounding, while budgets move annually and trust moves at whatever speed the last burned hand allows.

What the Analogy Doesn't Buy You

A 1905 motor did what its nameplate promised. A model's probabilistic; it can be confidently, fluently wrong, and an agent with hands needs controls a belt never did: identity, egress, metering, audit, a kill switch that means it. The governance plane comes first or the rewiring shouldn't happen at all.

Unit drive also minted a trade: the electrician who knew which machine earned its motor first. That's the trade I practice. We learn the workflow from the people who run it before there's a recommendation, and Island Mountain carries no catalog and no standing configuration, by design, so nobody here profits from selling you the biggest motor in the county.

Put a Motor on One Machine

So skip the bigger-motor bid this budget cycle; the unit-drive crowd had decades to be wrong first and you've got quarters. Pick the one workflow your people understand cold, the intake queue or the denial worklist, and put a motor on that machine: a governed model, owned weights, the floor rearranged so the work flows through it instead of around it. Measure what changed. Then rewire the next machine.

Summary: Factories that bolted one giant electric motor onto old line shafts saw marginal gains; the productivity came four decades later, from unit drive. A chatbot subscription laid over an unchanged workflow repeats that mistake on a capability curve that compounds in months.
