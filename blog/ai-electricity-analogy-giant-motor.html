AI Is Electricity in 1895, and Everybody's Shopping for the Biggest Motor in the County

Basho Parks

Most AI buying in 2026 repeats the 1895 factory mistake: one giant electric motor bolted where the steam engine stood, every shaft and belt left where it was.

The shafts had drawn the building around the engine's reach instead of the work's flow, and the motor changed nothing. Warren Devine's shafts-to-wires accounting puts electric motors under five percent of American factory drive in 1899 and near eighty by the late twenties; Paul David's dynamo paper traced productivity flat for nearly four decades. The gains didn't arrive until unit drive put a motor on every machine and let the layout answer to the work.

The standing play, 44 months into the chatbot era, is seat licenses over an unchanged workflow. MIT's NANDA group measured the wreckage last summer: 95 percent of enterprise generative-AI pilots returned nothing measurable to the P&L, generic tools draped over workflows that didn't change shape. The five percent getting paid embedded the system in one workflow and let the workflow reorganize around it.

That play treats AI as a chatbot you subscribe to. That's meeting electricity and concluding the product is lamps. The chatbot's a fine lamp; I burn one all day. But a seat can't hold your workflow, data or audit trail. Unit drive today is a governed model inside an operation whose data can't leave the building: open weights on hardware you own, held by controls in your paddock rather than a vendor's.

You don't get David's four decades. METR's task-length work has frontier agents doubling the length of work they finish roughly every seven months, and across 2024 and 2025 the doubling ran closer to four. Months, compounding, while budgets move annually and trust moves at the speed of the last burned hand.

A 1905 motor did what its nameplate promised; a model's probabilistic and can be confidently, fluently wrong. An agent with hands needs controls a belt never did: identity, egress, metering, audit, a kill switch that means it. The governance plane comes first or the rewiring shouldn't happen at all.

Skip the bigger-motor bid this budget cycle. Pick the one workflow your people understand cold, the intake queue or the denial worklist, and put a motor on that machine: a governed model, owned weights, the floor rearranged so the work flows through it instead of around it. Measure what changed. Then rewire the next machine.
