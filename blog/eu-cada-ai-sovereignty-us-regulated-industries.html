Europe Just Defined AI Sovereignty. The U.S. Is Still Pretending It Doesn't Apply to Us.

Basho Parks

Updated October 28, 2026: CADA is still a proposal under review by the European Parliament and the Council. Separately, the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July; it moved the AI Act's high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in Annex I regulated products, and left the Article 50 transparency duties on their original schedule. The sovereignty argument below doesn't depend on either date.

Europe just wrote down the thing American compliance teams have spent years refusing to say out loud, and it wrote it down in four numbered tiers.

On June 3, 2026, the European Commission proposed the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), COM(2026) 502. Parliament and the Council can still change it, but the shape's plain: a four-tier sovereignty framework for public-sector cloud and AI, with a top tier that shuts out any provider subject to foreign law. Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen said critical cloud providers shouldn't have a "kill switch," and U.S. companies would struggle to reach the top tiers because of the U.S. CLOUD Act (18 U.S.C. § 2713), which lets Washington compel American companies to produce data wherever it's stored.

Europe named it. I'm watching whether the American law firms, tribal governments, defense contractors and healthcare systems carrying the same exposure have noticed.

What CADA Proposes

Tier 1: infrastructure inside the EU, the floor. Tier 2: documented independence from third-country operational interference. Tier 3: EU ownership, control and personnel. Tier 4: supply-chain control with no third-country interference, hardware, software, people and legal jurisdiction all EU-originating, reserved for defense, law enforcement and border management.

The Commission puts U.S. companies above 70% of the EU cloud market and EU spending at €264 billion a year, mostly on U.S. proprietary IT. Whatever you make of Brussels, it put a number on the dependency.

The Same Exposure, Without the Vocabulary

CADA won't bind you, but it names the problem American regulated industries have tolerated for years: extraterritoriality. The CLOUD Act compels American providers to disclose customer data wherever it's stored. So when a tribal government uploads records to AWS or a defense contractor feeds CUI into a cloud LLM, that data sits where its own federal government can reach it through a provider-level order, without notice. Europe drew Tier 4 around exactly that. American regulators haven't, and the exposure's identical.

Tribal nations. The First Nations Principles of OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession) say a tribe owns its data, controls its use and physically possesses it. The CLOUD Act runs straight through Possession: a tribal government on a U.S.-headquartered cloud has surrendered possession by operation of law, without a council vote.

Law firms. ABA Model Rule 1.6 requires reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure of client information, and "reasonable" moves with what's commercially available. With local AI on the market, running privileged material through a third-party cloud service is a harder position to call reasonable than two years ago.

Defense contractors. ITAR (22 C.F.R. Parts 120-130) restricts exporting controlled technical data to foreign nationals. If a compelled disclosure touches a foreign-national employee or a foreign-jurisdiction data center, you've got an unauthorized export and an export compliance officer with a bad afternoon ahead. CMMC 2.0 requires CUI protection under NIST SP 800-171, and only air-gapped local infrastructure fully satisfies its isolation requirement.

Healthcare. 45 C.F.R. § 164.312 requires technical measures against unauthorized access to ePHI in transit. A Business Associate Agreement moves contractual liability; it doesn't move the data or physical control of it, and the CLOUD Act can compel ePHI whether or not there's a BAA in the drawer. The Commission's text says it outright: data transfer frameworks don't remove sovereignty concerns, because sovereignty goes beyond transfers and relates to operational autonomy.

Any major cloud AI service, OpenAI's API or AWS Bedrock, puts you at Tier 1 at best. The infrastructure may be domestic, but the provider's subject to U.S. law and under terms that'll change without asking you. Tier 1 doesn't hold for PHI, CUI, client confidences or tribal proprietary data.

Summary: The proposed Cloud and AI Development Act writes down what the strictest U.S. compliance frameworks require: physical control of protected data by the organization that owns it. Its Tier 4 would shut out any provider the CLOUD Act can reach; American regulators haven't, and the exposure's identical.
