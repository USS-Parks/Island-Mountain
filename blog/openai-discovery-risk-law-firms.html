OpenAI Discovery Risk: Why Law Firms Face Subpoena Exposure with Cloud AI

Basho Parks

Updated October 28, 2026: Magistrate Judge Ona Wang's May 13, 2025 preservation order required OpenAI to hold consumer ChatGPT output logs that would otherwise be deleted. It was lifted for most consumers in late 2025; enterprise and zero-retention API customers had been carved out. On January 5, 2026, Judge Sidney Stein affirmed an order requiring OpenAI to produce a sample of 20 million de-identified ChatGPT logs to the news plaintiffs.

Two federal courts in the Southern District of New York have made it plain: what your attorneys type into a cloud AI service is discoverable, retained on a stranger's docket and unprotected by privilege. Your clients' privileged disclosures sat on OpenAI's servers for months under an order your firm couldn't have stopped.

The Preservation Order

On May 13, 2025, Magistrate Judge Ona Wang issued a preservation order in In re: OpenAI, Inc. compelling OpenAI to preserve all output log data it'd otherwise delete, at user request or under international privacy law. (Hodder Law)

OpenAI normally purges deleted chats within 30 days. Under the order, manually deleted chats were held on OpenAI's servers instead (Harris Beach Murtha), across Free, Plus, Pro and Team, until the blanket hold lifted in late 2025. Enterprise got a carve-out early; most firms' people aren't on Enterprise.

A vendor's retention is governed by the vendor's docket, not your firm's; your litigation holds and ethics walls don't reach a server you don't own.

From Preservation to Production

The news plaintiffs asked for 120 million logs; OpenAI countered with 20 million, calling it "surely more than enough," and the plaintiffs agreed. OpenAI then tried keyword searches instead; Judge Wang sided with the news outlets and denied reconsideration in December 2025. (NatLawReview) On January 5, 2026, Judge Sidney Stein affirmed the order, and the 20-million-log sample is now in production to the plaintiffs.

Courts treat AI chats as discoverable business records; there's no special AI privilege (Terms), and none coming, because nobody's drafting one. The courts called "de-identified" adequate protection; I wouldn't bet a client relationship on it.

United States v. Heppner

On February 10, 2026, Judge Jed Rakoff took up "a question of first impression nationwide" and ruled that a defendant's exchanges with Claude were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine. (Harvard Law Review)

Bradley Heppner, charged with securities and wire fraud, had received a grand jury subpoena and retained counsel. Without attorney involvement, he used Claude to prepare reports outlining his defense strategy, then shared them with his lawyers. (Chapman and Cutler LLP) His attorneys logged them as privileged; the government moved to compel, and Rakoff granted it from the bench.

The government argued, and Rakoff agreed, that sharing privileged communications with a third-party AI platform may waive privilege over the underlying attorney-client communications. (Jones Walker LLP) The FBI seized the documents from his home; a defense strategy prepared for counsel became a prosecution exhibit. That's the structural problem I keep writing about.

The Shadow AI Problem Inside Your Firm

The ABA made the duty explicit in Formal Opinion 512, July 2024: attorneys protect client information regardless of the tool. More in my compliance briefs.

AI adoption among lawyers nearly tripled between 2023 and 2024, 11% to 30%, per the ABA's Legal Technology Survey. A paralegal might run a confidential memo through online AI for a spell-check (LeanLaw); that isn't misconduct and sits on nobody's risk register. Under the hold, that memo sat on OpenAI's servers until the order lifted, and nobody at the firm knew, least of all the paralegal, who was being careful.

OpenAI's and Anthropic's policies both permit disclosure in response to legal process, and opting out of training doesn't touch that. (Jones Walker LLP) Sam Altman warned that ChatGPT conversations aren't legally protected and that the court order then required OpenAI to retain chats, deleted ones included. (Kang Haggerty LLC) Good on him for saying it out loud. But an acknowledgment isn't a control. The preservation obligation attaches to whoever holds the data, so the fix sits where prompts never leave the building and the logs are yours alone.
