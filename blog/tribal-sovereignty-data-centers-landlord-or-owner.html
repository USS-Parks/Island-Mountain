Landlord or Owner: Tribal Nations, Data Centers, and the Sovereignty Question Nobody's Asking

Basho Parks

There are two seats at a data center deal and only one of them compounds. Landlord or owner. Everything else in the term sheet is detail.

The Pitch Arriving at Tribal Council

Over 100 hyperscale data center projects have been proposed on or adjacent to tribal lands in the past two years (Honor the Earth). The appeal's structural. Tribal lands sit where the wind and solar are. Reservation water supplies the cooling a hyperscale facility demands, between 300,000 and 5 million gallons per day (Futurism). And tribal sovereignty means permitting and environmental review look nothing like what the developer faces in suburban Virginia. Every one of those is a feature the developer extracts from the tribe's sovereign status.

The NDA Playbook

The pattern's documented across Indian Country (ICT News). A subsidiary or a Native-owned energy company approaches leadership about solar. Then the scope shifts to a hyperscale campus the solar will power, and before details get shared the corporation asks the council to sign a non-disclosure agreement. That's where the whole thing inverts: leadership now answers to a corporation's confidentiality terms instead of to its citizens, and council members who've signed can't tell the people who'll live with the consequences what's in it.

The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma got an NDA and a letter of intent from a tech startup, held an emergency town hall on March 3, 2026, and voted 24-0 on March 7 for a permanent moratorium on hyperscale data centers (Native News Online). Mekusukey Band Representative Glen Chebon Kernell: "Once we've used up and contaminated all of our water sources, this is it." The Muscogee Nation's National Council voted a proposal down 4-11 after citizens showed it'd sit on a 5,570-acre plot used for the nation's food sovereignty initiative (Moms Clean Air Force).

Landlord or Owner

The Department of Energy's February 2026 webinar title opened with "Beyond Land Leases" (DOE Indian Energy), which concedes the lease is the default and gestures at something better it never names. So let me name it.

The landlord position is where most proposals land. The tribe leases the land; the corporation builds, owns and operates, and the data, the models trained on it and the value the compute generates are all the corporation's. The tribe gets lease payments, some construction jobs and the water bill. That's the Dawes Act in digital form: a tribal asset converted into non-tribal wealth through a mechanism the tribe technically agreed to.

The owner position's one almost nobody proposes. The tribe builds or co-owns the infrastructure, decides what data runs on its land, and captures the value of the compute instead of the rent on the dirt underneath it. It can run tribal health data and emergency management operations on tribally owned hardware, beyond a CLOUD Act warrant's reach because no U.S. technology company ever takes custody of it.

The Cherokee Nation's taking the careful route, and good on them. Principal Chief Chuck Hoskin Jr. signed an executive order in February 2026 creating a nine-member task force to study data centers across the 7,000-square-mile reservation, report due June 30, 2026 (Cherokee Phoenix).

The Tribe's Own Compute

A data center's somebody else's data on your land; local AI's your data on your own hardware. I'd ask a council a smaller question first: what are our own compute needs, and are we meeting them? If tribal health services send PHI to cloud AI platforms, the tribe's already in the landlord position with its own information, paying a different corporation by the token.

An on-site deployment puts that inference inside the building, air-gapped if the posture calls for it, with no token meter. The OCAP Principles demand physical possession of tribal data; NCAI Resolution NC-24-008 defines digital sovereignty as authority over the data and the physical infrastructure it moves through. A tribally owned server on tribally owned broadband satisfies both. We sit next to the enrollment officer before recommending anything, learn the work the way she runs it, then pick a stack, deploy it on site and onboard the team.

Before Signing Anything

  • Who owns the infrastructure after construction? If the corporation does, everything after is rent.
  • What happens to the water? Five million gallons a day doesn't recharge the aquifer.
  • Who controls what runs on your land, and does any of it run the tribe's own work? Demand data governance provisions; a partnership with no tribal compute in it is a lease with better adjectives.
  • What's the exit clause? Twenty to thirty year terms outlast most councils and corporate commitments.

Every era of American expansion has offered tribal nations an "economic opportunity" that traded a sovereign asset for a revenue stream somebody else controlled: land for annuities, mineral rights for royalties. Data centers follow that pattern unless the tribe breaks it, and the only way it breaks is ownership: own the compute, own the data governance, own the server holding the government's most sensitive records. That last one's what we build. What it costs to give sovereignty away compounds forever.

Summary: Over 100 hyperscale data center projects have been proposed on tribal lands. The landlord seat repeats every previous era's extraction pattern; the owner seat captures the compute's value. On-premises inference on sovereign land satisfies both OCAP's Possession principle and NCAI Resolution NC-24-008.
