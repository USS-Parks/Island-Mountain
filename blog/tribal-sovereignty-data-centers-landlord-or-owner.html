Landlord or Owner: Tribal Nations, Data Centers, and the Sovereignty Question Nobody's Asking

Basho Parks

There are two seats at a data center deal and only one compounds. Landlord or owner; everything else in the term sheet is detail.

Over 100 hyperscale data center projects were proposed on or near tribal lands in two years (Honor the Earth). The land sits where the wind and solar are, reservation water covers the 300,000 to 5 million gallons a day hyperscale cooling demands (Futurism), and sovereign permitting looks nothing like suburban Virginia's. Every one's extracted from the tribe's sovereign status.

The NDA playbook's documented (ICT News): a solar pitch that grows into a hyperscale campus, then an NDA before the council sees details, so members who've signed can't tell the citizens what's in it. The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma got one with a letter of intent and voted 24-0 on March 7, 2026 for a permanent moratorium on hyperscale data centers (Native News Online). The Muscogee Nation's council voted its proposal down 4-11 once citizens showed it'd sit on a 5,570-acre food sovereignty plot (Moms Clean Air Force).

The landlord seat's where most proposals land: the tribe leases the land; the corporation builds, owns and operates, and keeps the data, the models trained on it and the compute's value. The tribe gets lease payments, construction jobs and the water bill. That's the Dawes Act in digital form, a tribal asset converted into non-tribal wealth through a mechanism the tribe technically agreed to.

The owner seat's one almost nobody proposes. The tribe builds or co-owns the infrastructure, decides what runs on its land, and captures the compute's value instead of rent on the dirt. Its health data and emergency management records run on tribally owned hardware, beyond a CLOUD Act warrant's reach, since no U.S. technology company takes custody. That server's what we build.

Before Signing Anything

  • Who owns the infrastructure after construction? If the corporation does, everything after that's rent.
  • What happens to the water? Five million gallons a day doesn't recharge an aquifer.
  • Does any of it run the tribe's own work? Without data governance provisions and tribal compute, it's a lease with better adjectives.
  • What's the exit clause? Twenty-year terms outlast most councils.

Every era of American expansion traded tribes a sovereign asset for revenue somebody else controlled; a lease does it again unless the tribe's in the owner's seat.
