OCAP Principles and the CLOUD Act: Why Tribal Data Requires Local AI Infrastructure

Basho Parks

Ask where your data lives and you'll hear about a data center. Ask who can be ordered to hand it over and the answer decides things: the vendor, and nobody tells the tribe. That's the law as written, not a risk I'm forecasting.

OCAP stands for Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession, First Nations' information governance standards set in 1998. (Wikipedia) Ownership: a community owns information about itself. Control: it governs how that information gets collected and used. Access: it can reach data about itself wherever it's held. Possession: it physically holds the data instead of handing custody to an outsider. Those rights sit inside First Nations' inherent treaty right to self-government (Springer), and Possession is the one the CLOUD Act collides with.

The 2018 CLOUD Act wrote down a different view of who holds what. 18 U.S.C. § 2713 makes a provider preserve or disclose data within its possession, custody, or control regardless of whether that data's inside or outside the United States. Ireland, Canada, a rack somebody's marketing as "tribal cloud," none of it changes the outcome, because data access follows corporate control, not information location. (archTIS)

A tribe whose data is held by a third party and seized by an outside government won't be told its data is under investigation, because it's no longer within its control. (Tgandh) Sovereign immunity doesn't cover it; the order goes to the technology company, which has no treaty relationship with the United States.

NCAI's resolution NC-24-008 defines digital sovereignty as "the exercise of sovereign authority over physical and virtual network infrastructure" and over the data that crosses it. (Gricnews) The word physical is the half of that sentence that costs money.

On-premises servers can be erected on tribal lands and run by the nation itself, and the up-front investment is hefty. (Brookings) Models running on site give no U.S. company custody, so § 2713 has nothing to reach. Cherokee Nation CIO Paula Starr has said a tool that compromises Cherokee values doesn't belong in the Nation's systems (Anadisgoi); that's a values test upstream of a procurement test, and I'd take that ordering over most enterprise architecture reviews.

I won't guess from a web page which box your nation needs; if you're looking at local AI, reach us and we'll learn the work on site first.
