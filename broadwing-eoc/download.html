County map data

The installer and the Mac app carry coarse elevation for the whole world, the statewide layers and the Humboldt, Del Norte and Siskiyou county packets, installed and checked on the first start. Every other California county comes as its own signed packet, with detailed imagery and elevation, buildings by use, raised at their heights when the map is tilted, county roads, street address search, and fire, flood, fault and weather zone layers. An administrator adds counties in Settings, Map data, or carries them in on a drive for a computer with no connection. Data release ca-2026.5.

Before you install

Check the file, then run it

Verify the download

Open PowerShell in the folder you saved it to and run the line below, with the file name of whatever you downloaded. The output has to match that file's SHA-256 above, character for character.

Get-FileHash '.\Broadwing EOC v2.5.7.exe' -Algorithm SHA256

On a Mac, in Terminal:

shasum -a 256 Open-Source-EOC-2.5.2-macOS-unsigned.dmg

If SmartScreen prompts

The Windows installer is code-signed, and Windows names Basho Parks as its publisher. SmartScreen may still flag a new release as unrecognized for its first downloads. Once the hash matches, choose More info, then Run anyway.

On a Mac, expect a Gatekeeper prompt

Drag Open Source EOC into Applications and open it. The app isn't signed by an Apple developer yet, so the first time, macOS says it couldn't check it: choose Done, then in System Settings, Privacy & Security, choose Open Anyway beside the line saying Open Source EOC was blocked. macOS asks only once. The first time you choose each mode, it takes a minute or two to set up.

Read me

Broadwing EOC is open source and built for local and tribal governments, healthcare providers, and NGOs where smaller groups staff the EOC and a single incident can cut internet connectivity for an already isolated jurisdiction. It runs on a Windows or macOS computer owned by the jurisdiction, using its own database, and is built to operate without an internet connection. Maps, imagery and elevation data are stored on the machine, and field forms can be filled out on a phone or tablet without a signal and sent once the connection comes back. A mobile version will be released in November, 2026.

Broadwing covers the day-to-day work of an EOC during a major emergency: incident boards, ICS positions and forms, and a map-centered common operating picture. A user manual is built in: press F1 on any screen to read that section's chapter, and Training & Exercise walks through each section step by step. Messages work as you expect, with a channel for each ICS section and unit and standing meeting rooms that join Teams, Zoom or Jitsi meetings. It also handles email and SMS notification with contact lists and call-down, reports, damage assessment, resources, JIC review, a record of every change, and federation, so two jurisdictions can share one incident while each runs its own server. It uses the standards EOCs exchange data in (CAP, EDXL, CoT, HAVE and GeoJSON). An IPAWS alert doesn't go out until a second administrator confirms it. Broadwing EOC is released under the Apache-2.0 license with no per-seat fees, and read-only viewers cost nothing. A Tribe's records and a county's incident history stay on hardware they control, not in a vendor's cloud data center.

Broadwing has no public-facing side. Every record is treated as For Official Use Only, so there's no anonymous map, public dashboard or citizen intake form. Mutual-aid partners get in through a guest grant that expires. It runs as one server process against one database rather than a load-balanced cluster. Live hazard feeds connect only when an administrator adds them; everything else on the map is stored on the machine. What a small to medium size Tribal, county, or municipality EOC gets today is an operations center it can install, own and run without a contract or a connection, along with a written record of what has been tested and what hasn't.

Privacy policy

Who publishes it. Broadwing EOC is published by Island Mountain AI Inc. and maintained by Basho Parks. This policy covers the Broadwing EOC app on Windows and macOS. It does not cover this website; the website has its own privacy policy.

We don't collect your data. Broadwing runs on computers your organization owns, with its own database. It has no Island Mountain account, sends no usage analytics or telemetry, uploads no crash reports, and doesn't check back with us for updates. Nothing you enter in Broadwing reaches Island Mountain, and we have no way to see it.

What the app stores, and where. Broadwing keeps what your organization and its people enter: names, email addresses and phone numbers of staff and contacts, positions, incident records, messages, files, photos, signatures and map locations. It stays in your organization's database on your own computer or network host. Passwords are stored only as one-way scrypt hashes. A phone or tablet used in the field keeps a copy of its queued work so it can work without a signal; when a device PIN is set, that copy is encrypted. Training & Exercise mode uses made-up exercise data only.

Location. The app reads a device's location only when a person asks it to, such as the location button on a field form or on the map, and only once each time. It doesn't track anyone.

Logs and crash reports. Broadwing keeps its logs and crash reports on the computer it runs on. Only your organization's instance administrators can view them. They can save them as a diagnostics file to share with someone, if they choose to.

Connections your organization turns on. With nothing switched on, Broadwing makes no connection beyond your own computer and network. These connect only when your organization's administrator or a user sets them up, and they go only to the service named:

Live hazard feeds from public sources such as the National Weather Service, the U.S. Geological Survey, NASA and CAL FIRE; camera layers from Caltrans and ALERTCalifornia; email through your organization's mail server, and text messages through its SMS provider; webhooks; collaboration channels and meeting links in Microsoft Teams, Zoom or Jitsi; incidents shared with partner EOCs your administrator approves; IPAWS alerts sent to FEMA; the audit log forwarded to your organization's own log server; and sign-in through your organization's identity provider. Each of those services handles what it receives under its own privacy policy.

County map data. When an administrator adds a county in Settings, Map data, Broadwing downloads that county's packet from Island Mountain's download server. Like any web server, it may record the requesting address and the time. Nothing else is sent, and packets can also be carried in on a drive with no connection at all.

Your organization controls the data. Your organization decides what is kept, sets how long records are kept, and can export or delete them. If you have a question about information about you held in an organization's Broadwing, ask that organization; Island Mountain has no access to it.

If you contact us. If you email us, or your organization sends us a diagnostics file for support, we receive what you send: your email address and what is in the message or file. A diagnostics file carries logs, crash reports and version details. Broadwing takes database passwords out of it, but it can still hold other information from that computer, such as names in log lines, so we treat it as confidential. We use it only to answer you and fix the problem, keep it only as long as we need to for that, and delete it sooner if you ask. We don't sell it or share it with anyone, apart from the email service that delivers our mail.

Your organization's role. Your organization runs Broadwing and decides how it is used. It is responsible for securing its computers, network and accounts, for its backups, for deciding who has access, and for telling its staff, volunteers and the public how it collects and uses their information, under the laws that apply to it, including public records laws.

Security. Broadwing protects what it holds with passwords stored as one-way hashes, encrypted connections between computers on a network host, roles that limit what each person can see and change, an audit trail of every change, and an encrypted copy on field devices that have a device PIN.
