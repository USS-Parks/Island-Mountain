Before you install

Check the file, then run it

Verify the download

Open PowerShell in the folder you saved it to and run the line below, with the file name of whatever you downloaded. The output has to match that file's SHA-256 above, character for character.

Get-FileHash .\Open-Source-EOC-Setup-2.5.0-unsigned.exe -Algorithm SHA256

On a Mac, in Terminal:

shasum -a 256 Open-Source-EOC-2.5.0-macOS-unsigned.dmg

Expect a SmartScreen prompt

The demo isn't code-signed yet, so Windows flags the installer as an unrecognized app. Once the hash matches, choose More info, then Run anyway.

On a Mac, expect a Gatekeeper prompt

Drag Open Source EOC into Applications and open it. The app isn't signed by an Apple developer yet, so the first time, macOS says it couldn't check it: choose Done, then in System Settings, Privacy & Security, choose Open Anyway beside the line saying Open Source EOC was blocked. macOS asks only once. The first time you choose each mode, it takes a minute or two to set up.

Version 2.5.0

Release notes

Open Source EOC is an amalgamation of popular emergency management software. It's open source and built for rural and remote local and Tribal governments, where one or two people staff the EOC and a single fire can cut the only fiber line into the valley. It runs on a Windows or macOS computer that the jurisdiction owns, with its own database, and it's built to run with no internet connection at all. Maps, imagery and elevation data are stored on the machine, and field forms can be filled out on a phone or tablet without a signal and sent once the connection comes back. A mobile version is planned for release in the coming week, and FEMA approval is expected soon. It covers the day-to-day work of an EOC: incident boards, ICS positions and forms, and a map-centered common operating picture. It also handles email and SMS notification with contact lists and call-down, reports, damage assessment, resources, JIC review, a record of every change, and federation, so two jurisdictions can share one incident while each runs its own server. It uses the standards EOCs exchange data in (CAP, EDXL, CoT, HAVE and GeoJSON). An IPAWS alert doesn't go out until a second administrator confirms it. It's released under the Apache-2.0 license with no per-seat fees, and read-only viewers cost nothing. A Tribe's records and a county's incident history stay on hardware they control, not in a vendor's data center.

Version 2.5.0 adds unified command. Several agencies can share one incident, each with its own standing, its own people and its own records, and each choosing who sees what it shares. They can work on one computer or on their own installs, connected over the network or by files carried on a drive, and keep working when cut off, with anything both sides changed listed for a person to settle. Plans, objectives and closing the incident are approved by every command agency. Standing mutual aid agreements bring their partners in with one action. A partner such as a food bank shares its sites with every incident it joins and keeps them current from its own system. A local EOC reports up to its operational area or the state, which sends its priorities back down. Federal CUI is marked and kept from anyone not cleared for it, and resource requests export on FEMA's form. The position job aids now fit any organization. Upgrading from 2.0.1 keeps every record.

Version 2.0.1 makes Open Source EOC an ordinary application on Windows and macOS. It opens in its own window instead of Chrome or Edge, carries everything it runs on, its own database included, and quits like any other app; on a Mac it no longer needs Postgres.app or Terminal. Upgrading from 1.3 keeps every organization's records, county packet and map. It also fixes saving a lifeline or ESF assessment on an exercise run by another organization, the column menus on the Chronology and other tables, which were cut off, and long lifeline names on the ESFs & Lifelines cards.

Version 1.3.3 fixes the app opening once and then showing “Failed to fetch” on later starts; it now opens every time. The Map draws in Open Source EOC's own colors and keeps your layer choices from one map to the next, the command position reads EOC Director, and fixes from an architecture review make sign-in renewal, record history, database upgrades and the map's refresh more dependable.

Version 1.3.2 makes Open Source EOC one app with two modes. It installs as a single entry in the Start menu and on the desktop, opens with no command window, and starts on a menu: Training & Exercise opens the four exercises on synthetic data with no username or password and every page open, and Real-life Operational keeps an organization's own records in their own database behind an email and password. Road closures and other road records now follow the real road. A closure can be drawn from two points or a rough loop around a stretch of road, a point can be snapped to the nearest road, and the tracing runs on the installed street map with no internet. The Map adds the 1,311 ALERTCalifornia cameras and 3,408 Caltrans highway cameras with a published position, which draw offline and, online, open on their current image or Caltrans live video. Upgrading from 1.3.1 sets the Training & Exercise data up fresh, keeping one backup, and doesn't touch an organization's own data.

Version 1.3.1 brings county map data. Each California county is now its own signed packet, with detailed imagery and elevation, buildings by use, county roads, street address search, and hazard layers: CAL FIRE fire hazard severity zones and fire perimeters back to 1878, FEMA flood zones, USGS faults, and National Weather Service forecast and fire weather zones. The installer and the Mac app carry the statewide layers and the North Coast counties, and an administrator adds any other county from Settings, or carries it in on a drive. Tsunami hazard areas are available as a live feed, since their publisher doesn't allow them to be bundled.

Version 1.3.0 is a security and reliability release, with the same layout. A full audit of the program found gaps in who can read and change what, and each one is closed: board fields only an administrator may set, restricted fields showing up in the chronology, search, shift handoffs and reports, what gets signed and sent to partner EOCs, and IPAWS confirmations, which are now tied to the exact destination the second administrator approved. On a shared computer, one person's offline data and sign-in stay out of the next person's. Offline work saved from two tabs at once is all kept, photos queued with a field report are uploaded when the console reconciles, and a failed map update, backup or host setup no longer leaves the machine worse off than it found it. Organizations can import and share their own map layers, a Tribe can show, hide or replace its own boundary, text is a pixel larger throughout, and the license text of every bundled library ships with the program.

Version 1.2.0 refines 1.1.0 in place, with the same layout. The icons are redrawn in color, a 3D terrain in contour lines sits behind the pages, and every map opens on imagery with roads and place names over it. With terrain on, the map orbits the way Google Earth's 3D view does. Wildfire feeds join the live hazard feeds: CAL FIRE's current incidents, NASA FIRMS satellite hotspots, NOAA smoke, and ALERTCalifornia's fire camera towers, whose live stills show on request with the program's credit. Nineteen position job aids, from the EOC Director to the unit leaders, are built in. The Overview and the Tasks, Resources, After Action Review and Damage Assessment dashboards show their counts across operational periods, the briefing view reads on a projector, and Settings adds a compact density and the Atkinson Hyperlegible typeface.

Version 1.1.0 rebuilt the map on the model of Esri's emergency management maps and added chart dashboards like WebEOC's. The map draws California's critical facilities by lifeline with an original set of 40 icons, tribal lands, FEMA's National Risk Index and the CDC's Social Vulnerability Index, and buildings colored by use or by public, private and critical-infrastructure role, with FEMA USA Structures occupancy across Del Norte, Humboldt, Siskiyou and Trinity counties. Fire perimeters, evacuation zones, road closures, shelters and damage each draw in their own style, solid or hatched, and every layer has its own opacity and fades in and out as it's switched. The legend lists only what's on the map at the current zoom, a click pages through every feature under it, terrain tilts into a 3D view of the ridges and canyons, a USNG grid is one switch away, and a writer can draw an area on the map to open a notification to the people in it. The Overview shows the same picture in miniature. Tasks, the IAP, Resources, After Action Reviews, Shelters and Damage Assessment each gain a dashboard of charts that filter the list beside them. On sign-in the console offers each person their ICS positions and opens the position's job aid, and an incident's partner organizations see everything the incident holds. Live hazard feeds from the National Weather Service, NIFC, USGS, NOAA and ORNL's outage data can be added in one step.

Open Source EOC has no public-facing side. Every record is treated as For Official Use Only, so there's no anonymous map, public dashboard or citizen intake form. Mutual-aid partners get in through a guest grant that expires. It runs as one server process against one database rather than a load-balanced cluster. Live hazard feeds connect only when an administrator adds them; everything else on the map is stored on the machine. Version 1.1.0 was the first full release, 1.2.0 refines it, and 1.3.0 closes what a full software audit found. Everything proven so far was done on the project's own machines with synthetic data, and live IPAWS, a load test on real deployment hardware and a first pilot jurisdiction still haven't happened. What a small Tribal or county EOC gets today is an operations center it can install, own and run without a contract or a connection, along with a written record of what has been tested and what hasn't.
